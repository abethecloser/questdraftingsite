If you have ever been handed a thick roll of drawings and told "this is your permit set," you would be forgiven for having no idea what you are looking at. A residential construction document set for a custom home in the Phoenix area typically runs around 22 sheets, and every one of them exists for a reason — either your builder needs it to build, or your city needs it to approve.

Here is what is usually inside, in plain terms.

## The cover and general sheets

The set opens with a **cover sheet**: the project address, a sheet index, the applicable codes, and often a rendered elevation so everyone knows which house they are looking at. Alongside it you will usually find a **general notes** sheet — the standing rules the whole set is built on.

## Site and civil

The **site plan** shows your home on your actual lot: property lines, setbacks, easements, driveways, and how the building sits relative to grade. In the desert, this is also where drainage and grading get resolved so water goes where it should. For many parcels this is the sheet plan reviewers scrutinize first.

## Architectural sheets

This is the heart of the set:

- **Floor plans** — every room, wall, door, and window, dimensioned.
- **Roof plan** — slopes, ridges, valleys, and drainage.
- **Exterior elevations** — what each side of the home looks like, with heights and materials.
- **Building sections** — vertical "slices" that show how the structure stacks up floor to roof.
- **Details** — the close-up drawings of the tricky connections.

## The supporting disciplines

A complete set is coordinated across roughly six disciplines. Beyond architectural, you will typically see **structural** (framing, foundations, beams), and depending on scope, **electrical, mechanical, and plumbing** layouts, plus **energy** documentation showing the home meets the current efficiency code.

## Why coordination is the whole game

Any one of these sheets is easy. The hard part — and the reason a permit set is worth paying for — is that they all have to **agree with each other**. A window on the floor plan has to match the elevation, the structure above it, and the energy calcs. When the sheets disagree, you get plan-review corrections, delays, and change orders during construction.
